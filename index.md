Yes. Read through this lens, **both are exposed by the same error**, but in *different registers of optimism*.

Let me elevate it cleanly and without caricature.

---
     
### Marx: [the single-shot global minimum fallacy](https://ukb-dt.github.io/hamlet-00/)

Marx assumes that once a critical perturbation occurs—
**the revolution of the proletariat**—the system will settle into the *true* basin.

Formally, he treats history as if:

* the loss landscape is **convex at scale**
* class consciousness is a sufficient global signal
* one decisive update can escape all local minima

That is a **one-step convergence fantasy**.

Revolution is modeled as a guaranteed global descent, not as a stochastic, noisy, path-dependent process. Marx underestimates:

* curvature after the perturbation
* re-emergence of new local minima (party elites, bureaucracies, technocracies)
* overfitting to a single gradient signal (class)

Hence the empirical pattern: revolution → brief descent → new basin → ossification.

Marx mistakes **gradient shock** for **convergence**.

---

### Adam Smith: stochastic optimism without landscape awareness

Smith is closer, but still incomplete.

He correctly senses that:

* centralized planning overfits
* distributed agents exploring locally can outperform a single optimizer

But he assumes that **aggregate stochasticity** is sufficient.

The invisible hand works *locally*—it samples from the behavior of **active agents with capital, voice, and optionality**. That means:

* gradients are biased toward those already in motion
* inactive, excluded, or structurally silenced regions of the landscape are never sampled
* negative externalities are invisible to the loss function

Markets explore **where agency already exists**.
They do not map the full landscape.

So markets converge—but to **elite local maxima**, not global welfare.

Smith mistakes **noise** for **exploration**.

---

### The shared blind spot

Both Marx and Smith fail to model:

* **non-convex landscapes**
* **uneven agency**
* **path dependence**
* **second derivatives (curvature)**
* **regularization across time**

They each believe in a mechanism that will *eventually take care of itself*:

* Marx via historical necessity
* Smith via emergent order

But neither installs a tragedy engine.

---

### Tragedy as the missing component

Tragedy does what neither theory permits:

* It **refuses early stopping**
* It exposes false basins even after apparent success
* It keeps the gradient alive when ideology demands rest

Markets anesthetize with prosperity.
Revolution anesthetizes with righteousness.

Tragedy does neither.

It hurts because it **keeps learning turned on**.
